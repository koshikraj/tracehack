 How a $5.3B Binance Hot-Wallet Hack Worked: EIP-7702, 50 Signatures and a 37-Minute Race
 # web3 
 # security 
 # ethereum 
 # blockchain 
 On 29 October 2026, attackers drained about $5.3 billion from Binance hot wallets across 10 blockchains in roughly 15 minutes . Binance puts the total impact at $6.3 billion , the largest crypto theft on record. About $2.7 billion has since been frozen or seized. About $2.5 billion escaped, most of it converted to ETH, which nobody can freeze. 
 I spent the morning after tracing it on Etherscan and through an independent tracker. This post walks through what is visible on-chain: the preparation, the EIP-7702 trick on Ethereum, why the evidence points at Binance's signing infrastructure rather than one leaked key, and how the attacker turned freezable stablecoins into ETH before Tether and Circle could react. 
 Caveat: figures are estimates as of 30 October 2026, ~04:30 UTC. Where a point is the tracker's own inference rather than something read off the chain, I say so.

 The attacker prepared for 18 hours : two sweeper contracts and successful rehearsals. 
 On Ethereum, one transaction carried 50 EIP-7702 authorizations signed by Binance 14's own key . One matched, and Binance's busiest hot wallet started running the attacker's code. 
 But Ethereum was not the entry point . XRP and Solana were drained first, and chains with five different key systems were emptied within minutes. That looks like a compromised signing or withdrawal layer, not a stolen key. 
 The attacker sold USDT at 4–22% below value to beat the freezes. 
 Background: what EIP-7702 changes
 EIP-7702 (shipped in Ethereum's 2025 Pectra upgrade) lets an ordinary externally owned account (EOA) sign an authorization : "run this contract's code as if it were mine." Once that authorization is included in a type-4 transaction, the EOA delegates its code to the contract. 
 Two properties matter here: 
 Only the EOA's own key can sign the authorization. Whoever submits the transaction (the sponsor) cannot forge it. 
 Each authorization is bound to one nonce. It only takes effect if the account's nonce matches at the moment the transaction lands. 
 So to hijack a wallet this way, you need its key to sign for you, and you need to hit its nonce. 
 Step 1: Preparation (18 hours ahead)
 The attacker's control wallet is labelled on Etherscan as "Binance Hot Wallet Exploiter" : 0x7C8601d4645b93cea45F71e118A729332e6B5CA0 . It was funded with about 1.4 ETH. Press reports say the setup money came through Tornado Cash.

 Step 2: The sweeper contract
 Sweeper v2 is 1,674 bytes of Solidity 0.8.28. Its source on Etherscan was reconstructed from bytecode by a third party, so the function names are guesses, but the behaviour is clear:

 The key detail is the hard-coded caller. Once a wallet delegates to this code, only the attacker can drain it . Nobody can front-run them and Binance can't simply call it back. 
 Step 3: The takeover (20:20:59 UTC)
 Binance 14 ( 0x28C6c06298d514Db089934071355E5743bf21d60 ) is one of Binance's busiest Ethereum hot wallets. It sends transactions constantly, so its next nonce is unpredictable. 
 The attacker's answer: 50 authorizations, one for each nonce from 18,914,775 to 18,914,824 , all signed by Binance 14's key and all pointing at sweeper v2, packed into a single type-4 transaction. 
 In that block, Binance's own transaction used nonce 18,914,808. The attacker's transaction landed right after, and the authorization for 809 matched. From that moment Binance 14 was running the attacker's code. 
 The sweep followed 3 minutes 24 seconds later .

 Two details stand out. 
 The Circle account. Binance 14 owned a Circle smart account (a single-owner modular account). Controlling the owner meant the attacker could call execute() on it and pull out 823M USDC. Circle froze the collector at 21:04:59. It later blacklisted the victim account too, because it still delegated to the attacker's code and kept receiving deposits. 
 Binance's automation kept feeding the hijacked wallet. The wallet that tops up Binance 14 sent its routine 200M USDT at 21:42:59, and the attacker swept it 72 seconds later. By then the collector was already frozen, so that USDT is stuck.

 Five different key systems were drained in one 30-minute window, mostly by ordinary signed transfers from what look like Binance's own hot wallets. One leaked private key cannot do that. 
 What can do it is a signer that signs whatever it is handed. That also explains the only exotic step: getting 50 valid EIP-7702 authorizations out of Binance 14's key. 
 This is consistent with: 
 Binance's statement that a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop may have let an attacker reach a service container and submit unauthorized withdrawals, with no evidence of a private-key leak. 
 Blockaid's preliminary view of "key misuse": the signing infrastructure was tricked, not the key extracted. 
 To be clear, this is an inference. An insider, or a compromised withdrawal-approval system feeding the signers valid-looking requests, fits the on-chain evidence equally well. That the non-Ethereum senders belong to Binance is also inferred, because those wallets are unlabelled. 
 Step 6: Racing the freezes
 USDT and USDC can be frozen by their issuers. ETH, BTC, XRP and SOL cannot. So the attacker's job after the drain was to convert as much as possible before Tether and Circle reacted. They had 37 minutes before Tether blacklisted the collector at 21:01. 
 Hop wallets. USDT moved through chains of fresh wallets. Tether blacklisted eight addresses between 21:01:35 and 21:18:59, but each hop was frozen only after the money had left it. 
 Chain A: collector → 0x9749...c762 (500M USDT). About 325M was sold via OTC and solver settlements. The remaining 175M was frozen at 0xaa24...54a0 . 
 Chain B: collector → 0x0ddf...697e (200M) → five more hops → 0xb171...c8c3 , where the last 30M was frozen.

 Lessons for builders
 A few takeaways for anyone running hot wallets or signing services: 
 EIP-7702 authorizations need their own policy. A signer that will sign a delegation tuple pointing at arbitrary code is effectively handing over the account. Treat delegation authorizations as the highest-risk message type: allow-list delegate addresses, and alert on any signing request for one. 
 Rate and shape limits on the signer, not just the wallet. Fifty authorizations across consecutive nonces in one request is an anomaly a policy engine could flag. 
 Assume your automation will keep feeding a compromised wallet. Top-up and sweep jobs should halt automatically when a hot wallet shows unexpected code (for example, a non-empty 0xef0100... delegation designator) or unusual outflows. 
 Watch for delegated smart accounts owned by your EOAs. The Circle account was drained through its owner. Compromise the owner, compromise everything it controls. 
 Freezes are a race measured in minutes. Issuers reacted in roughly 37 minutes; the attacker had already converted hundreds of millions. Pre-arranged emergency contacts with issuers and bridge operators matter. 
 What we still don't know
 How exactly the signatures were obtained, beyond Binance's "compromised engineer laptop" statement. 
 Who did it. TRM Labs, Blockaid and ZachXBT suspect North Korean groups such as Lazarus or TraderTraitor. That is an assessment, not a confirmed attribution. 
 Where the BTC chunks went. 
 Whether Tether will burn and reissue the frozen USDT, as it has done before. 
 Binance has suspended deposits and withdrawals and says it will cover all losses from its SAFU fund and company money. If you hold funds there, that's their promise, not a guarantee. 
 And a final warning: these wallets are now famous. Expect fake "recovery" services, people offering a cut of the money, airdrops from these addresses and links asking you to connect your wallet. Don't interact with any of them.
